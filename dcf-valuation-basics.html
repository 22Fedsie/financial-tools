How a discounted cash flow valuation works.

A discounted cash flow (DCF) model estimates what a business may be worth today by projecting future cash flows and discounting them to the present. The result depends heavily on assumptions; it is a framework for analysis, not an objective price.

From forecast cash flow to enterprise value

In an enterprise DCF, analysts forecast free cash flow to the firm (FCFF), which represents cash available to debt and equity investors after operating needs and reinvestment. The forecast cash flows are discounted using the weighted average cost of capital (WACC).

Core relationship: Enterprise value = present value of forecast FCFF + present value of terminal value.

The CFA Institute overview of free-cash-flow valuation describes the FCFF approach and the bridge from firm value to equity value.

Typical model steps

  1. Build a forecast for revenue and operating performance.
  2. Estimate FCFF after taxes and reinvestment. A common simplified structure is after-tax operating profit, plus non-cash depreciation and amortization, less capital spending and investment in working capital.
  3. Estimate a discount rate consistent with the risk and financing of those cash flows. WACC combines the cost of equity and after-tax cost of debt using their capital weights.
  4. Discount each forecast cash flow to today's value.
  5. Estimate value beyond the explicit forecast period with a terminal value, then discount that amount as well.
  6. Subtract net debt and other relevant claims from enterprise value to estimate equity value; divide by diluted shares for an illustrative value per share.

Terminal value and sensitivity

One common method assumes cash flows grow at a steady rate after the forecast period: terminal value = next-period FCFF ÷ (WACC − perpetual growth rate). The growth rate must be below WACC for this formula to produce a finite positive result.

Terminal value can make up a large share of estimated enterprise value, so small changes in WACC or long-term growth can move the result materially. A sensitivity table helps show a range of outcomes across assumptions; it does not tell you which assumption is correct.

Small illustrative example

Suppose a hypothetical company is forecast to generate FCFF of $1 million, $2 million, $3 million, $4 million, and $5 million over the next five years. With a 10% WACC and 2.5% perpetual growth assumption, the simplified Gordon-growth terminal value at the end of year five is about $68.3 million. Discounting the annual cash flows and terminal value gives an enterprise value of roughly $53.1 million.

This example is deliberately simplified. It does not estimate any real company, include net debt or share count, or establish a fair value. The terminal value accounts for about four-fifths of the illustrative result, showing how sensitive a DCF can be to long-run assumptions.

Common limitations

  • Forecasts can be wrong, especially for companies with unstable or negative cash flows.
  • WACC, terminal growth, and cash-flow assumptions are uncertain and can dominate the output.
  • Debt, leases, minority interests, stock-based compensation, dilution, and other claims may require careful treatment.
  • A standard FCFF and WACC approach may be less suitable for banks and insurers, where debt and regulatory capital are part of operations.
  • A DCF is one valuation approach. Market-based comparisons can provide a different perspective, but depend on the selected peer group and multiples.
