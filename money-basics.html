Financial literacy

Start with the idea behind the number.

These short guides explain concepts used in the calculators and Excel models. They link to Canadian public resources for details and current rules.

Paying down several debts

Write down each balance, interest rate, and minimum payment. Keep making every minimum payment. If an account is past due, review it first and contact the lender if you may miss a payment.

Avalanche: direct extra money to the debt with the highest interest rate. This can reduce total interest when rates and payments stay as assumed.

Snowball: direct extra money to the debt with the smallest balance. Paying off an account sooner may make progress easier to see, though it can cost more interest.

The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada guide to paying back debt describes both approaches. The free debt calculator estimates one balance; the private review workbook compares multiple debts using simplified monthly calculations.

Budgeting for a home

A mortgage payment is only one part of the cost of owning a home. Consider property taxes, heating, insurance, utilities, maintenance, and one-time closing costs such as inspection and legal fees. These expenses can vary by home and location.

The FCAC home-buying guide outlines mortgage preparation and additional costs. Compare estimates with local quotes and your lender’s terms.

RRSPs and TFSAs

RRSP contributions may be deductible, and withdrawals are generally taxable. TFSA contributions are not deductible, and qualifying withdrawals are generally tax-free. Contribution room and account rules matter, so check your own records before contributing.

The account is the container; the investment is what it holds.A bank may offer a deposit-style TFSA or RRSP with savings and GIC options. A self-directed plan can offer a wider selection. The choices depend on the plan and provider.

Common investment types

Both plans can hold many of the same eligible investment categories. Examples can include cash and deposits, GICs, mutual funds, most securities listed on designated stock exchanges (such as shares and ETF units), and certain bonds. Specific investments must meet CRA rules, and providers may limit what their plans offer. See the CRA’s qualified-investment overview.

Cash and savings deposits

Money kept as cash or in a deposit account. Check the interest rate, access rules, and account fees.

GICs and term deposits

A deposit held for an agreed term. Rates and access vary; some GICs cannot be cashed early or may have restrictions.

Bonds

A loan to a government or company. Some bonds qualify; value and risk depend on the issuer and terms, and a bond’s market price can change before maturity.

Stocks

Shares in a company. Some listed shares qualify, but their prices can rise or fall and depend on the company’s fortunes.

ETFs and mutual funds

Funds that pool investor money to hold investments such as stocks or bonds. Their holdings, risks, trading rules, and fees vary; an ETF is not automatically diversified or low risk.

Before choosing a fund, review its holdings, risk information, and fees. Read the fund’s ETF overview from Ontario’s investor education site or the relevant Fund Facts document. For current account details, see CRA guidance for self-directed RRSPs and permitted TFSA investments.

The site’s RRSP-versus-TFSA calculator compares simplified account estimates; it does not assess which investments or account are right for you.

Turn a savings goal into a plan

Choose a goal, write down an amount, and set a timeframe. Then compare the monthly contribution needed with what fits your budget. Review the plan as your priorities or circumstances change. The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada guide to savings and investment goals explains these steps and how timeframe can affect planning.

A projection is only a scenario. The Savings Goal Planner assumes a steady estimated return and regular month-end contributions; real returns vary, and fees, taxes, and personal circumstances can change the result. See the Savings Goal Planner review draft and its assumptions before using it.

Interest, growth, and assumptions

Interest rates, payment timing, fees, and changing returns affect estimates over time. An investment calculator shows a scenario based on the return you enter; it does not predict or guarantee future results. Review the calculator methods and assumptions before relying on an estimate.
